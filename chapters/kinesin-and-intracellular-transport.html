Summary
Just as goods must be transported from one point to another within a city, cells have developed roadways (cytoskeletal filaments), engines (motor proteins), and cargo containers (membrane organelles) to physically transport molecules. A failure of the biological transport system can lead to diseases of the nervous system as well as other organs. In this Key Discovery, we will explore how microscopes and test tube assay systems were used to tease apart how the cell's intracellular transport system works, which led to the discovery of a new motor protein: kinesin.

Introduction

Could a single evening in the laboratory change the course of one's life? This is the story of such an evening.

I was a 25-year-old student in a combined MD/PhD training program. For part of my PhD research, I was studying how neurons transport materials over long distances between the neuron cell body, where proteins and membranes are mostly synthesized, and the nerve terminal (Figure 1). This transport occurs through the axon, an extraordinarily long process whose length (up to 1 meter long for a motor neuron in your body) can be approximately a million times greater than its diameter. The axon interior is a busy highway specialized for transporting molecular goods.

What force propels the movement of building blocks (proteins and lipids) through the axon? This was the question that I was trying to answer in my last few months of my PhD work before starting the last clinical phase of my MD training. But none of my experiments were working. Were these experiments too difficult? Or was I just clumsy? It was tempting to give up. However, my fascination with the research kept me going. But the clock was ticking. I now had just three weeks left to wrap up my PhD research before transitioning to the clinic.

Then came a magical evening when my experiment finally worked. Before calling it a night and going home, I squeezed in one last "control experiment." A "control" checks the role of a particular variable in the outcome of an experiment. However, this control experiment led to something much more than a routine check. It generated a surprising result and created a route for discovering a new motor protein called kinesin.

Why would someone decide to "drop out" of medical school because of a control experiment? Why are unexpected results often the most important in science? Let's find out.

The Problem: How Is Material Transported in Nerve Cells?

As described in the Introduction, neurons transport materials (proteins and lipids) through the axon. The question is how. What is the motive force driving this transport?

When I began this research, there was considerable knowledge of some forms of biological motility such as muscle contraction and the beating of cilia and flagella, which will be described briefly in the Clues below. However, the mechanism of axon transport was unknown. This is what this discovery story is about.

Clues

Motility, an obvious attribute of living organisms, has been observed and studied for many centuries. These forms of eukaryotic movement involve actin filaments or microtubules, two fibrous polymers that comprise the "cytoskeleton." You can review the basics of the cytoskeleton in Video 1.

Clue 1: Discoveries of the First Motor Proteins, Myosin and Dynein

Walter Kühne, in 1864, extracted a viscous protein material from muscle and gave it the name "myosin." Later, in the chaotic midst of World War II in Hungary, Albert Szent-Györgyi discovered that Kühne's extract was actually composed of two proteins: the motor protein myosin and a fibrillar protein called "actin." When actin filaments and myosin were recombined in a test tube, they formed threads. Remarkably, when a solution containing ATP (adenosine triphosphate, a chemical energy source) was added, the threads shortened, analogous to the contraction of natural muscle fibers (Figure 2).

"To see them contract for the first time, was perhaps the most thrilling moment of my life." (Albert Szent-Györgyi)

Dynein was the next motor protein to be discovered in the early 1960s. Using an electron microscope, Ian Gibbons found that the interior of Tetrahymena cilia contained long fibrous proteins, which were later named "microtubules." Gibbons also could see little "arms" extending from one microtubule to a neighboring microtubule. He called this new protein "dynein" (dyne means "energy" in Greek), and later showed that dynein causes the sliding of adjacent microtubules in the cilia (Figure 3).

Clue 2: Transport Within Axons

Peripheral nerve damage was among the many devastating injuries suffered by soldiers during World War II. In an effort to understand and perhaps promote the regeneration of these nerves, Paul Weiss and Helen Hiscoe in 1948 surgically constricted the sciatic nerve of rats. After several weeks, they observed that the axon appeared swollen on the side of the constriction closer to the spinal cord. When the constriction was released, the bulge of material moved slowly down the nerve.

Later studies revealed two types of transport within axons: a slow transport of cytoskeletal elements and a much faster transport of membrane organelles. One type of membrane organelle is mitochondria, an organelle that produces ATP. Smaller membrane organelles, also called vesicles, function like delivery vans, transporting proteins and lipids to where they are needed in the cell.

Clue 3: Video Microscopy Allows Direct Visualization of Organelle Transport in Squid Axons

Many of the small membrane organelles traveling through the axons were too small to be visualized by the light microscopy methods of the 1970s. However, in 1982, Robert Allen developed a new technique called video-enhanced contrast microscopy that was capable of visualizing even the smallest membrane organelles inside cells (Figure 4).

Teaming up with Scott Brady and Ray Lasek at the Marine Biological Laboratory in Woods Hole, Massachusetts, Allen applied this microscopy to visualize the transport of membrane organelles inside of the squid giant axon (Video 3). For the first time, the busy highway of organelle motility inside the axon came into view.

◆ Explorer's Question
The American baseball player and comedic philosopher Yogi Berra once said, "You can observe a lot by just watching." What features of transport do you notice in this intact axon?
The organelles in an intact axon appeared to be moving along straight paths. Hence, scientists suspected that they might be transported along some type of cellular track. When the axoplasm was isolated, broken apart, and given ATP, individual tracks with moving membrane organelles became very obvious (Video 4).

Clue 4: Microtubules Are "Tracks" for Long-Range Axonal Transport

What were these "tracks" seen in Video 4? The light microscope lacked the resolution to tell whether they were actin bundles, microtubules, or a combination of the two. Using electron microscopy, Bruce Schnapp and Tom Reese found that the tracks had the diameter and periodic features characteristic of microtubules (Figure 5).

The Discovery: Kinesin, a Microtubule Motor Protein

With the "tracks" for axonal transport identified as microtubules, the next quest became identifying the motor. Trained as a biochemist, I decided to approach this challenge through "in vitro reconstitution." This approach is somewhat analogous to taking a car apart into its individual components and putting it back together, trying to use the minimal number of parts needed to make the car run again.

From the Clues described above, we knew that the parts list for axonal transport was: 1) microtubules (the tracks for movement), 2) adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the chemical energy source, and 3) membrane organelles (the cargos being transported), which we assumed had a motor bound to their surface.

Part 1: Reconstituting Axonal Transport In Vitro

Experimental failures are not necessarily "your failures"
Some days, the collecting boat failed to catch any squid. Other days, the microtubule preparation worked but the organelle preparation failed. Science requires a lot of persistence, and the process of trial and error is an inevitable part of science.

Finally, one day in the late summer of 1984, the preparation went well. After dinner, I had separate tubes of the prepared materials sitting in an ice bucket. I could now begin a series of experiments to try to reconstitute the motility of organelles along microtubules.

The First Experiment

My planned experiment was to mix together microtubules, ATP, and membrane organelles (Figure 6). After adding this mixture to a microscope slide, I focused the microscope with great anticipation. Would the axonal transport machinery come back to life?

Disappointing! Nothing happened.

The Second Experiment

I was taught that a good biochemist keeps all fractions in a biochemical preparation, just in case. So, during the preparation of the membrane organelles, I also kept a "soluble protein fraction," a collection of thousands of different kinds of proteins that are not associated with either organelles or cytoskeletal filaments. Since the first experiment didn't work, why not try adding it?

I was very excited by what I saw under the microscope. Now the organelles were moving along the microtubules!

The third (and fateful) control experiment

One nagging concern was that the moving objects were not the membrane organelles but some other particles from the soluble protein fraction. It was time to perform a control experiment.

◆ Explorer's Question
A positive control tests whether a variable is behaving as expected. What if a bad batch of ATP was the reason organelles were not moving? How would you design a positive control to test the ATP?
There are various ways in which this could be done. You need a well-established and reliable test that requires ATP. One could test the ATP solution on a well-established ATPase enzyme. Or one could have the ATP chemically analyzed.

In Experiment 3, I combined microtubules, ATP, and the soluble protein fraction, but left out the membrane organelles (Figure 8). The expectation was that I would see microtubules under the microscope but nothing moving along them. I looked under the microscope. Was I sleep deprived and seeing things?

The video camera revealed microtubules crawling along the glass surface like worms (Video 8). This mesmerizing and unexpected image was definitely not part of the game plan.

Molecular "crowd surfing"

Because the motors were stuck onto the glass and could not move, the microtubules were moving relative to the stationary motors. Think "crowd surfing." The rock star is transported by people who are largely stationary in the mosh pit (Figure 9).

After months of nothing working in the lab, lots of things were moving in a test tube within a few days. We now had lots of "assays" for detecting biological motility. An assay is an experimental method for measuring a biological activity.

Part 2: Purifying Kinesin

After these few, amazing days of experiments, I called Stanford to ask if I could postpone my return to medical school. I wanted to find the motor. To identify it, we had to purify a single protein from a complex mixture of thousands using column chromatography (Figure 11) and analyze the fractions by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Figure 12).

What Happened Next?

Several months later, we found that kinesin moves cargo from the cell body to the nerve terminal (anterograde transport). A couple of years later, using the in vitro motility assays described here, Richard Vallee identified the retrograde motor as a cytoplasmic version of dynein.

In the subsequent decade, work from many scientists revealed that kinesin is not a single motor but a family of related motor proteins. Humans have 45 different kinesins that are involved in development, cell division, and cell organization, in addition to axonal transport. Genetic mutations in kinesin are now known to be responsible for certain neurodegenerative diseases.

Closing Thoughts

The "scientific method" is often presented in textbooks as a flow chart beginning with a hypothesis followed by an experiment that proves that hypothesis right or wrong. This linear and logical path fails to capture the twists, turns, and surprises that often occur in research. Our experiment was designed to reconstitute the movement of organelles along microtubules, but the most exciting result, microtubules moving on glass surfaces, was unplanned and unexpected.

It is very difficult to envision the unknown, until one bumps into it. Scientific research, by its nature, probes the murky interface between the known and the unknown. Bumping into something new usually proves to be more interesting than the original intended journey.

What happened next in my career after discovering kinesin? I never did return to medical school. There were always more questions that stimulated my curiosity, and never a convenient time to stop.
